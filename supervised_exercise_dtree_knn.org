* Scenario

A tutoring centre keeps records of students who sat a certification
exam. For each student it recorded two whole numbers:

- $x_1$ — hours studied during the week before the exam (rounded to
  the nearest hour)
- $x_2$ — number of full practice exams completed

Each student is labelled with the outcome:

- *1 (failed)* — drawn as an open circle labelled "1"
- *2 (passed)* — drawn as a filled circle labelled "2"

A new candidate has just registered. They studied *4 hours* and
completed *3 practice exams* ($x_1 = 4$, $x_2 = 3$). This candidate
is marked with a diamond ("?") on the scatter plot below. Your job
is to predict, using two different methods, whether this candidate
will pass or fail.

No calculator is needed anywhere in this exercise: the scatter plot
is drawn on a grid with the same scale on both axes, so every
point's coordinates can be read off directly, and distances between
grid points can be compared by eye or with simple whole-number
arithmetic.

* Solutions

** Part A

The three closest training points to $(4, 3)$, found by counting
grid squares (or comparing $(\Delta x_1)^2+(\Delta x_2)^2$, which
needs no square roots to rank):

| Point   | $\Delta x_1$ | $\Delta x_2$ | Squared distance | Distance | Label |
|---------+--------------+--------------+-------------------+----------+----------|
| $(4,2)$ |            0 |            1 |                 1 |        1 | 1 (fail) |
| $(6,3)$ |            2 |            0 |                 4 |        2 | 2 (pass) |
| $(2,4)$ |            2 |            1 |                 5 | $\approx 2.24$ | 2 (pass) |

The next-closest point, $(4,6)$, has squared distance $0^2+3^2=9$
(distance $3$) — clearly farther than the three points above, so
there's no ambiguity about which three are nearest.

With $k=3$: two neighbours are "passed", one is "failed", so k-NN
predicts *pass*.

With $k=1$: the single nearest neighbour is $(4, 2)$, labelled
"failed", so the prediction flips to *fail*. This illustrates that
small $k$ makes k-NN sensitive to individual (possibly noisy)
points, while larger $k$ smooths the prediction by averaging over a
bigger neighbourhood — at the cost of possibly including less
relevant, more distant points.

** Part B

Path: $x_1 = 4 < 5$ → "yes" (go left); $x_2 = 3 < 3.5$ → "yes"
(go left). The candidate lands in the leftmost leaf, which predicts
*1 (fail)*.

That leaf is "pure enough" to be built from mostly class-1 points
(3 failed vs. 1 passed in the training data, see the lower-left
region of the scatter plot); a leaf predicts whichever class is the
*majority* among the training points that fall into it, even if a
few points of the other class are also present. Those minority
points are simply misclassified by the tree. The same is true of
the other two leaves — each is dominated by one class but contains
one or two points of the other.

Among the $x_1 \geq 5$ students, both the low-practice ones ($x_2 <
5$: one failed, two passed) and the high-practice ones ($x_2 \geq
5$: one failed, two passed) are already *pass*-majority. Splitting
on $x_2$ there would just divide the passing students into two
groups that are *still both pass-majority* — it wouldn't isolate any
additional failed students, so it doesn't make the tree any more
accurate. A tree only introduces a split when it actually separates
the classes better; here, once $x_1 \geq 5$, practice-exam count
stops being useful for predicting the outcome, so a well-built tree
leaves that side as a single leaf.

** Part C

1. No — k-NN ($k=3$) predicts *pass*, while the decision tree
   predicts *fail*. They disagree because the candidate lies close
   to two of the tree's splits at once ($x_1 = 4$ is only $1$ away
   from the split at $x_1 = 5$, and $x_2 = 3$ is only $0.5$ away from
   the split at $x_2 = 3.5$), and both of the regions just across
   those splits are pass-majority: a small, essentially arbitrary
   sliver of training data sends the tree one way, while k-NN instead
   looks at whichever labelled points happen to be geometrically
   closest, and two of the three closest happen to be "passed".
2. Here the fit is actually good, in the sense that matters: the
   region where *fail* is the more common outcome is exactly the
   bottom-left rectangle (low hours *and* low practice, $x_1 < 5$ and
   $x_2 < 3.5$) — everywhere else, *pass* is the majority, even though
   a few individual failed (and passed) students appear scattered
   outside their region's majority as noise. Because that
   majority-outcome boundary is itself an $\text{AND}$ of two
   axis-aligned conditions, a tree built from horizontal/vertical
   splits can match it exactly; the scattered noise points are
   exceptions that no boundary shape could capture anyway. That would
   not be true of every dataset: if passing instead depended on, say,
   $x_1 + x_2$ exceeding some diagonal threshold, no finite set of
   horizontal/vertical splits could match that boundary exactly — it
   could only be approximated by a "staircase" of small rectangles.
3. k-NN is a "lazy" method: it stores the entire training set and
   does no work until a new point arrives, at which point it must
   compute the distance from the query to every stored training
   point (or use an index structure) and pick the $k$ smallest.
4. Advantage of k-NN: it makes no assumption about the shape of the
   boundary between classes — it would work just as well if the true
   rule were diagonal or curved, where axis-aligned splits could only
   approximate it. Disadvantage: it must keep all training data around
   and recompute distances for every new prediction, which is slower
   at prediction time than descending a small, precomputed tree —
   here, and in general, whenever the true rule genuinely does align
   with the axes, a small tree is both cheaper and no less accurate.
